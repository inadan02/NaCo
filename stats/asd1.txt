It is a truth acknowledged by most young ladies of fortune and gentility, that a visit to Bath, while rarely prescribed by a physician in earnest, is among the most effectual remedies for languor of the spirits and discontent of the mind. Whether it be the fresh air that stirs upon the Crescent, the regularity of dining and dancing which the season provides, or merely the satisfaction of being admired by strangers, there is, among those of delicate constitution, no tonic so restorative to the frame, nor any diversion so constant to the thoughts. Miss Eleanor Willoughby, whose family resided chiefly in the county of Hampshire, had arrived in Bath under the recommendation of a change of scene, though none could precisely declare what she was to recover from, unless it were a disappointment of the heart, which her sisters hinted at but never named.

Miss Willoughby was the eldest of four daughters born to Mr. Willoughby of Langton Hall, a gentleman of old lineage and moderate estate, who, possessing neither the vivacity of youth nor the vigour of great ambition, had raised his children with an attention at once affectionate and negligent—allowing them books, horses, and opinions, while denying them London, governesses, and grand acquaintances. The result was a household in which music was practised with more enthusiasm than skill, novels read with more feeling than discretion, and conversation held with more wit than propriety. Mr. Willoughby’s sons (for he had two by a previous marriage) were by this time established elsewhere: the elder, a clergyman in Lincolnshire, and the younger, an officer stationed in Ireland, neither of whom had been at Langton Hall for some years, and whose correspondence with their sisters was brief, correct, and without incident.

Eleanor, at seven and twenty, was considered by many to be past the most promising season of matrimonial hope. Not that she lacked merit; indeed, her beauty, though not of the striking sort, was enhanced by a countenance so expressive of good sense and sweetness that few could converse with her long without finding her handsome. Her manner, too, was composed and elegant; and if her wit was sometimes too pointed, it was never ill-natured. But Eleanor had, in her twentieth year, received the attentions of a gentleman of such excellent character, whose family and fortune were entirely suitable, that when their apparent attachment came to nothing, the neighbourhood had no choice but to ascribe the event to some painful secret, perhaps an insurmountable obstacle, which delicacy forbade her to disclose. And so it was with a kind of solemn resignation that people ceased to speculate on her prospects and began to speak of her with that mixture of admiration and commiseration which is reserved for those who are considered, unjustly or not, to have missed their chance.

It was under these circumstances that Eleanor accompanied her youngest sister, Lydia, to Bath, under the guardianship of their aunt, Mrs. Godwin, a lady of such unwavering regard for fashion and form that, though her health scarcely permitted her to cross a drawing room without assistance, she never failed to deliver lectures on deportment from her couch with a vigour that would have astonished her apothecary. Mrs. Godwin, who had lived in Bath these fifteen years, considered herself a connoisseur in all matters of propriety and a person of consequence among the better sort. Her acquaintance, though numerous, was carefully regulated; she did not countenance tradesmen or their wives, however genteel, and she measured a lady’s merit by the quality of her cap, the neatness of her card, and the number of bows she received during a promenade.

It was at one of these celebrated promenades on the Royal Crescent that Miss Willoughby was introduced to Mr. Henry Seaborne, a gentleman of easy fortune and indolent manners, who had lately come into possession of a small but handsomely situated estate near Tunbridge Wells. Mr. Seaborne was of a countenance that bespoke more refinement than strength; his air was that of a man who had been praised too often for saying too little; and his conversation, while genteel and agreeable, was so carefully unexceptionable that it rarely approached the territory of either opinion or passion. Nevertheless, he was considered by many ladies to be very charming, and by some gentlemen to be very tiresome. It was not long before Mr. Seaborne’s attention to Miss Willoughby became evident to all who cared to observe such matters, and Mrs. Godwin, whose penetration was equal to her vanity, soon pronounced that the affair would end in a proposal, unless some foolish romantic notion on Eleanor’s part interfered with her better judgment.

The truth was that Eleanor, though flattered by Mr. Seaborne’s attentions, was not particularly touched by them. She admired his manners, appreciated his civility, and approved his moderation; but her heart, if it was not still under the quiet dominion of some former attachment, was at least too well guarded by experience and reason to be overpowered by compliments and dances. She had been too long accustomed to weigh character, and too often disappointed by appearances, to be easily won. It was not that Mr. Seaborne lacked good qualities, but rather that he appeared never to have exercised them in any cause more vigorous than the arrangement of his waistcoat or the selection of a carriage. He spoke of his travels with a kind of elegant indifference, and of his friends with an unimpassioned civility; and though he was never guilty of rudeness, he was equally incapable of enthusiasm.

Still, Eleanor did not discourage him. She had learned the necessity of moderation in all things, including disapproval, and was unwilling to repel a man whose intentions might yet become serious. She observed him carefully, listened to him attentively, and awaited some occasion on which he might betray a genuine feeling or opinion. Such an occasion came at last, though not in the manner she had anticipated.

It was during an evening at the Pump Room, when conversation, having exhausted the weather, the water, and the assembly, turned upon a recent pamphlet discussing the education of women. One lady, a Miss Dorothea Chilton, declared it was shocking that young ladies were taught little beyond dancing and drawing, and insisted that every girl should be well-read in history, mathematics, and the classics. Another, Mrs. Bransby, laughed and said that a little French and a good hand at backgammon were all the accomplishments required to keep a husband in good humour. The debate grew lively, and Mr. Seaborne was at last appealed to for his opinion.

He smiled, adjusted his cuff, and said, “I believe a lady ought to be intelligent enough to appreciate a good remark and gentle enough never to make one.”

Eleanor could not help but laugh, though she stifled it quickly.

“Then, sir,” said she, “you would require of a woman both understanding and silence—a curious combination.”

“I find it ideal,” replied he. “For what is harmony in a household but the suppression of discord? And how better can one ensure that, than by preferring sweetness to sense?”

“I believe the two are not mutually exclusive,” she said, “and I have often thought that a man who desires only a woman who agrees with him might be better suited to solitude.”

There was a moment of quiet amusement among the listeners, and Mr. Seaborne, to his credit, coloured slightly and did not attempt a rejoinder. But from that evening, Eleanor found her regard for him diminished—not by the opinions he expressed, which were common enough, but by the complacency with which he held them. She began to see in him not merely a lack of passion, but a want of principle, a tendency to glide through life rather than meet it. He admired elegance, but not excellence; civility, but not conviction. And though he continued his attentions, she received them now with an increased reserve, and answered his gallantries with a calmness that bordered on indifference.

Mrs. Godwin, upon perceiving this alteration, blamed Eleanor’s pride, her intellect, and her stubborn romanticism. “You shall never be married,” said she, with an air of tragic certainty, “because you will not be content with common happiness. You seek a man of sense, of character, of passion—but such men are rare, my dear, and do not always wear clean gloves. Mr. Seaborne is respectable. He is solvent. He has a phaeton. What more can you require?”

“I require,” replied Eleanor, with a smile, “that he should not prefer a woman who is intelligent enough to admire him but silent enough never to correct him.”

In the end, Mr. Seaborne proposed—and was refused. He bore the disappointment with a grace that confirmed Eleanor’s judgment; and though he left Bath a week later, he spoke of her in terms of high regard, saying only that she had a mind too strong for domestic tranquillity. Eleanor, for her part, resumed her walks, her novels, and her cheerful indifference to matrimony. And if she sometimes sighed over the memory of a man more deeply felt than remembered, she did so with the dignity of one who has loved wisely, if not well.

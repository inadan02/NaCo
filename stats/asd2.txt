The morning sun had hardly dispersed the silver tinges of dawn that lingered over the woods of Brookfield when Mrs Hartwell, who never allowed either weather or inclination to interfere with her habitual inspection of the household, descended to the breakfast parlour with the same air of moderate importance that distinguished her at all seasons. She was a lady of considerate temper, conscious of her duties as the mistress of a country manor and mother of five children, yet inclined, by a natural warmth of spirit, to invest every trivial occurrence with a dignity almost alarming to the inexperienced. That morning, however, a certain secret satisfaction shone through the precise regularity of her movements and betrayed the joyful agitation of an event expected. Her daughters, though not yet summoned, were scarcely less alert; for an express had arrived the preceding evening, giving notice that Sir Vincent Farnham, a gentleman of large fortune and unimpeachable connections, had accepted an invitation to remain a fortnight at Brookfield on his return from the sea coast, and the pleasure of such distinction could not be enjoyed in silence.

Eleanor, the eldest Miss Hartwell, possessed that composure of mind, united with acuteness of observation, which secured for her the respect of every acquaintance. At one and twenty she had acquired a reputation for prudence, possibly beyond her years, yet enlivened by a wit so playful that even her gravest reproof could seldom be received without delight. Marianne, who followed her by little more than twelve months, was of a spirit far more impetuous. She loved, with unwearied ardour, every elegant pursuit, and would listen to no doctrine of propriety that might check the instantaneous indulgence of feeling. Catherine, their younger sister, had not yet quitted those years in which imagination supplies the place of experience, and a propensity for romantic conjecture rendered her alternately credulous and incredulous, delighted with improbable good and alarmed by improbable evil. Edward, the only son, was pursuing his studies at Cambridge, and the youngest of the family, Julia, a lively girl of thirteen, was still under the tuition of a governess, though she thought herself exempt from all control but her own caprice.

The company at Brookfield, as soon as the arrival of Sir Vincent became known, multiplied more rapidly than Mrs Hartwell, with all her readiness to receive neighbours, had foreseen. Colonel Ashby, an old friend of Mr Hartwell, wrote to request a bed for three nights, and Mr Wentworth, the curate of Redford, found in the impending festivities a necessity for cultivating that acquaintance on which his comfort in the parish so materially depended. Mrs Dawkins, the widow of an officer and cousin to Mrs Hartwell, very willingly undertook the fatigue of a journey from Bath, accompanied by her daughter Lucy, whose taste in music was talked of wherever her person was introduced, and whose talent for saying pleasing things was valued even by those most apt to condemn the vanity of female accomplishments. These and several other visitors, whose names hold little consequence in the narrative, produced a variety of hopes and fears, of bustle and anticipation, such as render a country house vastly agreeable to the unoccupied, though often oppressive to the diligent or the diffident.

Breakfast on the appointed day had been prolonged beyond its usual limit by conjectures on the time of Sir Vincent’s arrival, when the sound of wheels, approaching with an air of deliberate grandeur, occasioned an instantaneous pause in conversation. Mrs Hartwell rose, adjusted a ribbon whose perfection needed no adjustment, and prepared to receive her guest in the hall with that union of ceremony and cordiality which she believed inseparable from true elegance. Eleanor, in a quieter manner, collected the apprehensions of her sisters, and endeavoured to diffuse a calm which she hardly felt. Every eye was directed towards the entrance; every pulse quickened with expectation; and when at length the tall figure of Sir Vincent crossed the threshold, attended by a valet and followed by trunks uncommonly large, the collective sigh, half of admiring surprise and half of timid scrutiny, might have gratified a greater hero.

He was a man of perhaps thirty four, tall, well proportioned, and though not handsome, the regularity of his features, joined to an expression of mild composure, conveyed immediate confidence. His address wanted neither grace nor ease, yet the total absence of officious gallantry gave an impression of steadiness that some ladies pronounced alarming. Mrs Hartwell, whose notions of refinement admitted nothing abrupt, welcomed him with an attention so regulated, so gently encouraging, that his acknowledgments could not fail to be pleasing. He spoke little, however, and with a gravity that seemed habitual. Eleanor observed him earnestly; Marianne admired him unreservedly; Catherine, disappointed that his coat, though faultlessly cut, could not be called magnificent, searched in vain for some token of romance.

A walk round the shrubbery had been proposed as soon as Sir Vincent had partaken of slight refreshment, but the plan, like most plans suggested under the influence of ceremony, was rendered impossible by a sudden shower. Its violence, though short, drove the whole party to the library, where each was compelled to assume an occupation. Mrs Hartwell, never at a loss, placed herself at the writing table and arranged letters to friends who required no answer, merely to mark her industry. Colonel Ashby discussed the comparative merits of modern cavalry with Mr Hartwell, whose taste for the army was speculative rather than practical. Mrs Dawkins attacked a portfolio of prints, Lucy seated herself at the pianoforte, and Mr Wentworth, after many expressions of concern for detained walkers, stationed himself beside her, ready to turn pages with a devotion uncommon in so young a clergyman.

The three sisters, with Julia, were assembled at one of the windows, apparently engaged with an album of botanical sketches, though in truth far more occupied in contemplating their distinguished guest, who had retreated to a corner furnished with books of history, from which he selected one and began to read with an earnestness unfeigned. Marianne, unable to remain tranquil, sought to draw him into conversation by observing, rather more audibly than was necessary, on the beauties of the surrounding scenery viewed from that particular window. Eleanor, perceiving her design, would have checked it, but Sir Vincent, closing his volume, joined them at once, and gave so rational a praise to the prospect as convinced Eleanor that his silence was the fruit of thought, not of indifference.

Catherine ventured a remark on the prospect of Redford Hall, whose turrets, half visible through the haze, presented to her lively fancy the image of chivalrous adventures. Sir Vincent smiled at her enthusiasm, but encouraged her observations, and by easy questions, led her to consider how far her admiration rested on the genuine charms of the landscape, and how far on associations of romance. Catherine felt both flattered and instructed; Eleanor was pleased with the balance of his politeness; Marianne, mortified that the conversation had not adopted a tone of brilliant sensibility, consoled herself by resolving to draw him out on poetry at the first opportunity.

Dinner, announced at the proper hour, brought no diminishment of the interest felt by every branch of the society. Mrs Hartwell’s table exhibited a profusion calculated to gratify a larger party than her own, yet arranged with that exactness which she had long considered the distinctive symptom of genteel establishment. Sir Vincent spoke little during the first course, but his attention to the ladies at either hand admitted neither remissness nor ostentation. By degrees, Colonel Ashby, whose spirits rose with the elegance of the second course, succeeded in engaging him upon his travels; he had returned but lately from Italy, and a few leading questions sufficed to introduce topics of architecture, painting, and the intermingled destinies of republics and princes. His descriptions, delivered without parade, yet with a precision that seemed to render the scene visible, fixed the notice of the whole table, and Mr Hartwell, a lover of the fine arts, declared, when the gentlemen rejoined the ladies in the drawing room, that he never before had conversed with a traveller who united so perfectly the curiosity of an observer and the moderation of a philosopher.

Tea and cards followed, in true country fashion, though Marianne lamented that music was postponed to so late an hour. Eleanor, watching Sir Vincent while she replied to the civil yet unremarkable speeches of Mr Grey, a neighbouring baronet who had joined them for the evening, observed an expression of thoughtful concern upon his countenance whenever he looked towards Catherine or Julia. It puzzled her; she could not be satisfied till she discovered the cause. Presently, when the groups had settled into rubber and speculation, she approached him with some casual enquiry drawn from his conversation at dinner, and in a tone of confidential persuasion, asked whether anything had occurred to diminish his amusement. He disclaimed every unpleasant feeling, yet his smile wanted animation. With graceful hesitation, he confessed that the sprightliness of the younger Miss Hartwells rendered him anxious lest their openness should one day be misunderstood by worldlier observers. Eleanor, grateful for so delicate an anxiety, assured him that her sisters were more rational than their vivacity might announce and that their principles, if not yet perfected by experience, possessed a solidity which time would strengthen. Sir Vincent, relieved, expressed himself satisfied, and Eleanor, though tempted to rally him on his premature solicitude, admired the benign spirit from which it sprung.

In the course of the week many entertainments succeeded each other with a rapidity worthy of London. A ball at Redford Hall supplied Marianne with occasion for exhibiting both her graceful person and her taste in lively conversation. She was universally admired, and an acquaintance of two hours convinced her that the homage of Mr Baldwin, a gentleman with four thousand a year, was something far superior to the qualified civility of Colonel Ashby or the silent observation of Sir Vincent Farnham. Eleanor, who judged not so confidently on so slight a foundation, believed Mr Baldwin agreeable yet superficial and cautioned her sister against hasty conclusions. It was received as caution usually is, with a smile of pity for the coolness of superior wisdom.

Sir Vincent, at the same ball, stood partnerless for several dances till reminded by Mrs Hartwell that Eleanor was disengaged. He offered his hand with frank politeness, and their conversation, during the figure, far exceeded the trivial texture usually woven at such times. They discussed the comparative effect of large assemblies and limited circles on social character, the felicity of country living when regulated by taste and the impossibility of preserving tranquillity where vanity presided. Eleanor declared herself an advocate for a moderate mixture of society, free from either the bustle of an overgrown neighbourhood or the apathy of solitude. Sir Vincent agreed, yet observed that the choice, though apparently simple, was seldom made without sacrifice; those who desired consequence sought a crowd in which to display it, while those whose pleasure lay in reflection sometimes forgot that the mind, like a field, required open breezes and sunshine, not continual shade. Eleanor listened, half smiling, half serious, and thought that if his reflections sprang from past disappointment he had borne it nobly.

Catherine meantime had fallen under the instruction of Mr Wentworth, whose knowledge, though far less extensive than he assumed, delighted her by its zeal. He recommended histories, plans of study, moral treatises, and she accepted the list with such grateful humility that his vanity was amply repaid. Edward, returning unexpectedly from college at the end of the first week, introduced new animation. He was full of academic anecdotes and earnest for every rural amusement uniting exercise with laughter. Sir Vincent joined him in shooting and riding; their acquaintance ripened daily, and Edward soon pronounced him the most rational companion he had ever met either in hall or field.

During one of these expeditions they called at Dawlish Lodge, the residence of Lady Kendal, an aunt of Sir Vincent. Her ladyship, though advanced in life, retained a vivacity of manner and a clearness of judgment which rendered her conversation universally desirable. She received Edward with frank good nature, congratulated him on his recent honours at the university, and tasked Sir Vincent to detail his impressions of Brookfield society, a subject on which he avoided particularity with an address demonstrating equal policy and kindness. Lady Kendal, with a playful shake of the head, declared that he must not expect to escape censure by mere silence. She flattered herself, she said, that her nephew, though naturally reserved, could not witness so much youthful grace without throwing his decisive influence on the side of happiness, and she should watch him narrowly.

Eleanor, when informed of Lady Kendal’s pleasantry, laughed yet blushed. The notion of Sir Vincent as decisive influence must imply something more than general benevolence; yet she instantly recalled her imagination from an excursion so flattering. Marianne, on hearing of the same conversation, was seized with astonishment that her own fascinations had altogether escaped mention. She deemed Sir Vincent insensible, yet was not displeased; his insensibility left her free to cultivate the admiration of Mr Baldwin, which, in her romantic predilection, had fixed upon her as upon a heroine.

A succession of mild autumn days invited frequent strolls. One afternoon, while Mrs Dawkins and her daughter were practising a duet, and the greater part of the gentlemen were otherwise engaged, Eleanor found herself on a shaded path by the brook, attended only by Sir Vincent. The situation, so accidental yet so secure from interruption, disposed them to confidence. They conversed on the pleasures of literary retirement, and gradually, by some imperceptible transition, on the deeper interests of domestic felicity. Sir Vincent spoke of the power of early impressions, of the necessity of sympathy in every friendship that claims duration, and of the rare but inestimable union of taste and principle. Eleanor listened, alternately delighted by the elegance of his manner and subdued by the seriousness of his sentiments. When they returned to the house she was conscious of having communicated more of her regard for rational excellence than she had ever before revealed to any one beyond her own family, and the tremor of that consciousness attended her throughout the evening.

Marianne meanwhile pursued her projects with undiminished ardour. She alternately tantalised and encouraged Mr Baldwin, believing every variation of her spirits a lawful resource in the display of irresistible charm. Colonel Ashby, who had cherished a sort of half serious preference for her since their first meeting, began to suffer from an unwilling jealousy, and even Mr Wentworth, whose clerical solemnity never wholly subdued his sensibility, felt aggrieved that so many captivating attentions should be reserved for a stranger. Lucy Dawkins, with soft concern, lamented that a disposition so brilliant should border on caprice. Mrs Hartwell saw, with maternal anxiety, that the gaieties supplying amusement to her second daughter might border on imprudence, yet the difficulty of interference increased each day; for Marianne, conscious of approbation wherever she moved, construed the most delicate hint into the severity of tyranny.

At length, on the second Thursday of Sir Vincent’s visit, a large party assembled for dinner, and in the evening a private concert was to be performed. Sir Vincent had previously engaged Eleanor as his partner for a country dance to follow the music, and a thousand conflicting hopes mingled in her bosom as the hour approached. Mrs Hartwell, desirous of appearing equal to every demand of hospitality, exerted herself with double energy; the servants flew in obedience; the children, even Julia, seized with a sense of general importance, obeyed her directions. When the dining room door opened, displaying a table whose decorations might have contented a countess, Mr Hartwell could not refrain from paying his lady a compliment aloud. Mrs Hartwell coloured with pleasure, and Sir Vincent, observing her sensibility, recommended moderation by a smile of respectful sympathy.

During dinner Sir Vincent’s attention seldom wandered from Eleanor. He addressed her with a tenderness veiled in the formality suitable to their acquaintance, but intelligible to both. Eleanor, though her voice sometimes faltered, sustained the conversation with a composure that concealed the agitation of her heart. Marianne, seated between Mr Baldwin and Mr Wentworth, found abundant triumph in every glance of interest bestowed upon her by the former. Colonel Ashby, condemned to the lower end of the table among elderly ladies, fortified his good humour by a double allowance of wine, and Mrs Dawkins lost no opportunity of determining, by delicate questions, the dimensions of Sir Vincent’s estate, the number of his tenantry, and the particulars of his carriage.

After dinner the company returned to the drawing room, where the instruments were already arranged. Lucy Dawkins opened the concert with an air on the harp. Her fingers, light and rapid, drew admiration loudly expressed. Marianne succeeded at the pianoforte with a sonata of fashionable difficulty, playing with all the fervour of early enthusiasm. Sir Vincent’s eyes were frequently fixed on Eleanor, whose taste, though cultivated, had never been ostentatious. He entreated a song. The entreaty, mild yet earnest, prevailed. She chose an air of Handel, serious, sublime, and sang with a clear sweetness that penetrated every ear. The room was silent till the last chord died away, when Sir Vincent, in a voice tremulous with emotion, thanked her for a pleasure which, he declared, had surpassed all former enjoyment of music.

The country dances began. Sir Vincent, fulfilling his engagement, led Eleanor to the top of the set. Their movements were in perfect harmony, and their spirits, each alive to the consciousness of being admired, animated the whole line. Marianne, with Mr Baldwin, danced opposite. Her vivacity, so bright and unreserved, enchanted most young men in the circle, and she alternately excited the envy of many ladies and the applause of all gentlemen. Catherine, though less conspicuous, enjoyed herself with naive delight, and Julia, permitted after long entreaty to join the younger set, displayed a simplicity that softened the ridicule usually attached to juvenile ambition.

In the pauses of the dance Sir Vincent ventured a confession of the regard which Eleanor had long suspected. He spoke with an eloquence springing from real feeling, yet restrained by that fear of disturbing her tranquillity which bespoke the true delicacy of his attachment. Eleanor, touched, grateful, yet aware of the necessity of caution, acknowledged an esteem too sincere to be concealed, while declining every explicit avowal till further intimacy with his character should justify so decisive a preference. Sir Vincent received her reserve with gratitude, not disappointment, and promised, in few words, that no earthly consideration should induce him to forfeit the blessing of her friendship. They parted with increased confidence; Eleanor’s heart throbbed, yet was at peace.

Marianne’s evening closed less auspiciously. Mr Baldwin, wearied by her perpetual demand for admiration, hinted at the charms of female modesty with a dryness that provoked her indignation. She retorted with sanguine vivacity, and he, piqued by her readiness, addressed his next compliment to Miss Dawkins. The retribution was keen. Marianne restrained her mortification, but Eleanor, when they retired to their chamber, saw tears that scorned concealment. Tenderly she soothed her sister, yet reminded her of the danger in every triumph sought rather than bestowed. Marianne listened in mingled pride and penitence, but the impression, though strong, could not yet conquer a temper so elastic.

The fortnight of Sir Vincent’s stay drew to its close. On the morning of his departure he requested a private audience with Mr Hartwell, the result of which was an immediate summons of Eleanor. When she entered the library she perceived in her father’s smile an unspoken benediction. Sir Vincent, advancing with respectful earnestness, repeated his solicitude for the privilege of dedicating his life to her happiness and placed in her hand a letter from Lady Kendal containing warm assurances of approbation. Eleanor, her eyes fixed on her father, beheld an expression of encouraging affection that dissolved her last hesitation. She accepted Sir Vincent’s devotion with a gratitude as profound as it was tender; Mr Hartwell joined their hands in solemn blessing; and the happiness of both seemed secure.

The intelligence spread through Brookfield with rapid speed. Mrs Hartwell, though not surprised, was overpowered by joy; her maternal ambition, always subordinate to her maternal tenderness, was at once gratified and ennobled. Catherine predicted numberless adventures for her sister in the elevated sphere to which she would belong. Julia rejoiced without prediction, and Edward, transported, declared that no alliance could have given him equal pleasure. Marianne, sincerely attached to Eleanor, forgot for an hour her own vexations, but a remembrance of Mr Baldwin soon intruded. Colonel Ashby, in confidential discourse with Mr Wentworth, lamented that Sir Vincent had been too quick for him, yet owned that the superiority of Eleanor might well excite a fervour no delay could diminish.

The wedding was fixed for the latter end of October, Lady Kendal having insisted that it should take place at Brookfield, where she was determined to preside and from which no persuasion could detach her till the ceremony should be accomplished. Preparation began on a scale less splendid than commodious, for Eleanor, moderate in desires, discouraged every attempt at imposing display. Sir Vincent, rejoicing in her preference, submitted to her economy with an alacrity that rendered his boundless generosity even more apparent.

During these arrangements Marianne received, through Mrs Dawkins, information of Mr Baldwin’s engagement to a lady of high rank. The blow, though softened by her pride, pierced deeply. She retired to her apartment, reviewed her whole conduct, and when Eleanor, alarmed, hastened to comfort, she burst into tears of bitter self reproach. Her sister entreated her not to view in unfortunate events an irretrievable humiliation, but rather a wholesome discipline. Marianne listened, and at length, yielding to that calm reason which Eleanor so gently enforced, resolved to substitute for the restless pursuit of admiration a cultivation of mind and heart more likely to ensure permanent esteem. Her reform was sincere; its effects, though not immediate to the eye of the world, gradually appeared in a sweetness of temper less susceptible of offence, and in an exertion to please without display. Colonel Ashby, still her admirer, perceived the change with delight and awaited an opportunity to renew his addresses under auspices more favourable.

So much of human felicity, however, as depends on the arrangement of external events seemed now assembled at Brookfield that quiet resumed its rightful influence. The bustle of novelty subsided into domestic order; Sir Vincent, in attending to the repairs of his estate, remained near the family; Eleanor, assured of his affection, enjoyed a serenity unknown till certainty replaced conjecture; Marianne occupied herself in music and charity; Catherine persisted in her romantic flights yet mingled them with serious reading; Julia improved daily beneath the mild vigilance of Lady Kendal. Mr Wentworth, charmed by the essay of Marianne’s reformation, renewed his attentions with zeal, and Mrs Hartwell, who found contentment in projecting future unions, anticipated another wedding.

When October arrived, mellow and bright, the marriage of Eleanor and Sir Vincent was solemnised in the village church, under an arch of oak and laurel prepared by the tenants of Brookfield and Farnham Park. The ceremony, simple in itself, derived dignity from the evident happiness of the parties and the grateful congratulations of a neighbourhood truly interested in their welfare. The bride, lovely in modest attire, leaned on the arm of her husband with a composure that spoke the fulness of her heart; he, steady in expression yet radiant in look, seemed to regard her as the pledge of every earthly blessing. Their departure for Farnham Park was attended by smiles and tears commingled, and Mrs Hartwell, when the carriage disappeared among the elms, acknowledged that she now understood the perfection of maternal joy.

The history of Marianne’s feelings during the following weeks might furnish a curious record of conflicting pride and tenderness, but this narrative, already extended beyond ordinary limits, must restrain itself. It is sufficient to say that Colonel Ashby, by gentle perseverance and evident esteem, secured a regard more solid, if less dazzling, than any her fancy had ever projected; Catherine, by a union of imagination and prudence, escaped the common dangers of credulous sensibility; Edward distinguished himself at the bar; Julia, under Eleanor’s affectionate counsel, grew into a woman scarcely less dignified than her eldest sister; and Mr and Mrs Hartwell, surrounded by prosperous children, experienced that tranquil satisfaction which, though rarely commemorated in the annals of romance, is perhaps the most delicious condition allotted to humanity.

If the felicity of the Farnham family needed further increase it received it from the conscious persuasion that their own happiness contributed to that of others. Sir Vincent, guided by Eleanor’s discriminating benevolence, reformed abuses on his estate, encouraged industry, and promoted education among the poor. The influence of their example extended beyond their immediate circle; neighbouring families, animated by the spirit of unaffected virtue, relinquished frivolous rivalry for emulation in acts of useful kindness; even the fashionable visitors from town, who paid their annual homage to rural novelty, returned to London with a conviction that elegance is never so irresistible as when allied to benevolence.

Thus, in scenes removed from magnificent parade, where taste directed rather than displayed opulence, and where affection sanctified every enjoyment, life proceeded with that gentle progress which loses nothing of real delight because unaccompanied by public acclamation. The youthful hours of expectation were succeeded by the mature season of gratitude; lively hopes terminated in even livelier content; imagination ceded its throne to reason without dethroning happiness; and the circle of domestic virtue, once formed, widened continually, embracing with silent energy all who approached it.

Let those who, delighting in the glare of splendid misfortune, doubt whether prosperity can engage the heart, examine the calm records of Brookfield and Farnham. They will perceive virtues strengthened, tastes refined, attachments confirmed; they will acknowledge that felicity, when founded on principle, is as capable of enlarging the mind as sorrow, and infinitely more productive of general good. Above all, they will learn that the truest trials of character are often found, not in the turbulence of calamity, but in the tranquil discharge of daily duties; that the mild light of domestic life, though undazzling, reveals the finest features of the soul; and that genuine content, resting neither on vanity nor on illusion, is accessible to all who seek it in the union of reason, tenderness, and incorruptible integrity.
